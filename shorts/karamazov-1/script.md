# The Brothers Karamazov, Part 1: "A Murder in the Family"

Draft 1 for review. ~2,150 words of narration, about 15 minutes.
Lines in [brackets] are picture notes, not narration. *Starred* words get the gold emphasis.

---

## Intro

[Blender: dark room, a sealed envelope on a table, candle flickering. Slow push in.]

A father is murdered in his own house.

An envelope with three thousand roubles in it, sealed three times, is lying torn open on the floor. On it, in his own handwriting: "To my angel Grushenka, if she will come." She never came.

And he had three sons. Every one of them had a reason to want him dead.

[Card: THE BROTHERS KARAMAZOV]

This is *The Brothers Karamazov*. Sigmund Freud called it "the most magnificent novel ever written." It's also one of the most *unfinished* books on anyone's shelf: around nine hundred pages, every character with three different names, and long arguments about God right when you just want to know who did it.

So let's read it together, in three parts. Today: the family, the night of the murder, and the question that turns a crime story into the deepest thing Dostoevsky ever wrote. I'll keep the killer for Part 3.

Because by the end, you'll see that the real question was never *who* killed Fyodor Karamazov.

It's who is *responsible*.

---

## Chapter I: The Father

[Chapter card: I · The Father]

What kind of man do all of his children want dead?

[Portrait of a 19th-century Russian landowner; close-ups of a wine glass, a laughing mouth.]

Meet Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov. He's rich, he's loud, and he is the most shameless man in his little provincial town. He started with almost nothing and got rich the way a certain kind of man does: by marrying well, borrowing cleverly, and never once feeling embarrassed about anything.

He had two wives. He made both of them miserable. And when they died, he did something almost worse than cruelty. He *forgot* his children existed.

The boys were raised by a servant, then by relatives, then by strangers. Not because their father was poor. Because he simply didn't think about them.

Think about that for a second. Most villains in novels hate someone. Fyodor doesn't hate his sons. He just never bothered to love them. And Dostoevsky is going to show us that this kind of absence can do as much damage as a blow.

[Statement card: "He didn't hate his sons. He just never bothered to love them."]

He's also funny. That's the uncomfortable part. Fyodor is a clown. He plays the fool on purpose, because if you're already laughing at yourself, nobody can humiliate you. Early in the book, the family meets at a monastery so that a wise old monk, Elder Zosima, can settle a fight over money. Fyodor turns the whole meeting into a circus: he lies, he jokes, he insults everyone, and he enjoys every second of it.

Now, here is something most readers never learn. In 1839, Dostoevsky's own father died suddenly on his country estate. For the rest of Dostoevsky's life, the story in his family was that the old man had been killed by his own serfs. It was never proven. But forty years later, his son sat down and wrote the most famous story ever told about a murdered father.

[Pause. Painting of a Russian country estate.]

Freud noticed that too. We'll come back to him.

## Chapter II: Three Sons, Three Selves

[Chapter card: II · Three Sons, Three Selves]

If you split one human being into three people, what would you get?

[Blender: three candles on one table, each burning differently: one roaring, one steady and cold, one small and calm.]

Dostoevsky's answer is the three Karamazov brothers. And the trick of the book is that they aren't really three people. They're three parts of *you*.

The oldest is **Dmitri**. He's the body and the heart. A former army officer, he's passionate, generous, violent, ashamed, and then passionate again, all before lunch. He spends money he doesn't have, falls in love too fast, and says exactly what he feels the moment he feels it. He's that friend you'd trust with your life but never with your credit card.

And he has two big problems. First, he's sure his father cheated him out of his mother's inheritance. Second, he and his father are in love with the *same woman*: Grushenka, a young woman the whole town gossips about. The father has an envelope of three thousand roubles waiting for her. Dmitri owes almost exactly that amount to his fiancée, and he's already spent it.

[Map-style card: Dmitri → 3,000 roubles ← Father, with Grushenka between them.]

The middle son is **Ivan**. He's the mind. Brilliant, cold on the outside, and tormented on the inside. He writes articles about religion that everyone argues about. And he has one idea that the whole novel orbits around: if there is no God, and no life after death, then there's no real reason to be good. In his words, "everything is lawful."

[Statement card: "If there is no God, everything is permitted."]

Notice what kind of idea that is. It's not a gun. It's not a threat. It's just a sentence said at a dinner table. Keep it in mind, because in this book, a sentence turns out to be the most dangerous weapon in the house.

The youngest is **Alyosha**. He's the soul. Twenty years old, gentle, and living at the monastery under Elder Zosima. He's not naive. He sees exactly how broken his family is. He just refuses to stop loving them. Dostoevsky actually calls him the hero of the book, which is strange, because for long stretches, Alyosha barely does anything. He listens. He shows up. He forgives.

And there's something you should know about that name. In May 1878, Dostoevsky's three-year-old son died of epilepsy, a disease he inherited from his father. The boy's name was Alyosha. Dostoevsky, grieving, travelled to a famous monastery, Optina, looking for comfort. A few months later, he began this book. He gave its gentlest character his dead son's name.

[Slow pan across an icon or monastery painting. Music drops.]

So: Dmitri feels everything. Ivan thinks everything. Alyosha loves everything. Which one are you?

Most of us are all three, fighting inside the same skin. That's why this family feels so familiar.

## Chapter III: The Fourth Son

[Chapter card: III · The Fourth Son]

But what about the son nobody counts?

[Painting: Kramskoi, *The Contemplator* (1876). Slow push in on the figure.]

There's a fourth young man in the house. His name is **Smerdyakov**, and he works as the family's cook. Everyone in town believes he's Fyodor's illegitimate son, born to a homeless woman the town called "Stinking Lizaveta." Even his last name is built from that insult. He grows up in the kitchen, watching his brothers from the side.

He's quiet, proud, resentful, and he suffers from epileptic fits, the same illness that killed Dostoevsky's son, and that Dostoevsky himself had all his life.

And here's one of my favorite details in the whole novel. To describe Smerdyakov, Dostoevsky stops the story and points to a real painting. This one. *The Contemplator*, by Ivan Kramskoi.

A peasant stands alone on a road in a winter forest, completely still. He looks like he's thinking, but he isn't, exactly. He's *absorbing*. Storing up impressions he couldn't explain if you asked.

Then Dostoevsky says something chilling. A man like this might one day drop everything and walk to Jerusalem to save his soul. Or he might set fire to his own village. "And perhaps do both."

[Statement card: "He might walk to Jerusalem. Or burn down his village. Perhaps both."]

That's Smerdyakov. A sponge, sitting in the kitchen, soaking up everything the clever people say at the table. Especially Ivan.

So the family isn't really three brothers. It's four. And the one nobody takes seriously is the one who's listening most carefully.

## Chapter IV: The Night of the Murder

[Chapter card: IV · The Night]

So what actually happened that night?

[Blender: night exterior feel, a dark window, a garden fence, moonlight. Slow, tense camera.]

Let's lay out the evidence the way the police would.

Dmitri is desperate. He has to pay back the three thousand roubles, and he's tried everyone in town. Days earlier, in front of witnesses, he burst into his father's house, knocked him to the floor, and shouted that he'd come back and kill him.

Then comes the night. Dmitri is convinced Grushenka might be with his father. He grabs a brass pestle, a heavy kitchen tool, and climbs over the garden fence. He creeps up to the lit window. He sees the old man inside, waiting for her, ridiculous and hopeful.

And then Dostoevsky does something brilliant. The narration *cuts away*. We don't see what Dmitri does at that window. The chapter just stops.

[Hard cut to black. Two seconds of silence. Whoosh.]

The next thing we see, Dmitri is running. The old servant Grigory grabs his leg on the fence, and Dmitri strikes him with the pestle and leaves him bleeding in the grass.

A few hours later, Dmitri, who had no money that morning, is throwing *thousands* of roubles around at an inn in a nearby village, buying champagne for everyone, and finally hearing Grushenka say she loves him.

Then the police walk in. Fyodor Karamazov has been found dead. The envelope is empty. And Dmitri is arrested for murder.

[Statement card: "The threat. The pestle. The blood. The money."]

Honestly? If you were on the jury, you'd convict him in ten minutes.

Dmitri swears he didn't do it. He says the money he spent was his own, half of the three thousand he'd sewn into a little cloth bag and carried around his neck for a month, out of shame. Nobody believes him.

But Dostoevsky has been careful. He never showed us the moment. He made *us* fill in the blank. And most of us filled it in exactly the way the police did.

Which tells you something about how quickly we decide who's guilty.

## Chapter V: Everyone Wanted Him Dead

[Chapter card: V · Everyone Wanted Him Dead]

So who's guilty?

[Blender: the envelope on the table again, now torn. Camera rises slowly over it.]

Here's where the novel gets uncomfortable. Because once you step back, you realize every son had a hand in this.

Dmitri *wished* for it, loudly, in public, many times. He hated his father enough to imagine killing him.

Ivan *thought* it. He spent the whole book arguing that without God, nothing is forbidden. And on the day of the murder, knowing that something terrible might happen in that house, he packed his bags and left town.

Even Alyosha, the saint, has a moment where he was supposed to watch over his brother, and got distracted by his own grief.

And Smerdyakov, the son in the kitchen, was listening to all of them.

That's the real idea at the center of this book, and it comes from the old monk, Zosima: *each of us is responsible for everyone*. Not in a vague, poetic way. Literally. The things we say, the things we wish, the moments we look away, they all go out into the world and land somewhere.

[Statement card: "Each of us is responsible for everyone."]

Freud read it very differently. In 1928, he wrote an essay called "Dostoevsky and Parricide," arguing that the novel is about a guilty wish buried in every son: to replace the father. You don't have to buy the psychoanalysis to feel the point. The murder in this book isn't just one man's crime. It's a family's.

And it's a question for us too. You've probably never wanted anyone dead. But have you ever said something carelessly, and later learned that someone took it seriously?

## Outro

[Blender: back to the candle on the desk. Slow pull out.]

At the front of this novel, Dostoevsky placed a single verse from the Gospel of John:

"Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit."

[Card with the verse, John 12:24.]

Something in this family has to die before anything can grow. Their father is dead. But the story is only beginning.

Dmitri is in a cell, swearing he's innocent. And the most dangerous person in the family never touched a weapon. He just had an idea, and somebody was listening.

In Part 2, we'll hear that idea in full: Ivan's rebellion against God, and the story he tells his little brother about Christ returning to earth and being arrested by the Church. It's called "The Grand Inquisitor," and it might be the strongest argument against God ever written.

[Card: PART 2 · THE GRAND INQUISITOR]

Which raises the question this whole book is really asking:

If there is no God, is everything permitted?
